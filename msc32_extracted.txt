Summary
Text entry is still a major problem in virtual reality (VR). Virtual QWERTY keyboards
use a layout that many users already know, but typing in VR can still be slow,
inaccurate, and tiring. VR systems use different input methods, such as controller
pointing, dwell input, swipe input, and hand tracking. However, past studies often use
different hardware, tasks, keyboard designs, and measures. Because of these differences,
their results are difficult to compare directly.
To make the comparison more consistent, this project developed a VR typing
application in Unity with six input methods. Press uses a controller ray and trigger to
select keys. Dwell uses a controller ray and a fixed wait time. Swipe uses a controller to
draw a path across the keyboard. StickTap uses direct controller contact with the keys.
HandTouch uses two tracked index fingers, while HandTouch10 uses ten tracked fingers.
All six methods use the same virtual QWERTY keyboard, target phrases, display, and
timer. The application records typing speed, text errors, corrections, and input actions.
It also collects user ratings for workload, fatigue, ease of use, comfort, satisfaction, and
preference.
Ten participants tested all six methods in a fixed order. The results showed clear
differences in typing speed, accuracy, workload, and user ratings. Each method had its
own strengths and limits. The main contribution of this project is the development of a
working VR typing application that includes six input methods in one shared test and
data-recording system. By using the same test conditions for all methods, the
application provides a common platform for comparing VR text-entry methods.

Chapter 1
Introduction
1.1 Introduction
1.1.1 Virtual Reality and Its Applications
Virtual reality, commonly abbreviated as VR, refers to a computer-generated
environment in which users can perceive and interact with simulated content through
devices such as head-mounted displays, motion controllers, and tracking sensors. Unlike
Augmented Reality, which overlays digital information onto the physical environment,
VR generally immerses users within a fully simulated environment.
Modern immersive VR systems combine head-mounted displays, motion tracking and
interactive input devices. Display resolution, refresh rate, field of view, tracking quality
and ergonomic interaction are important technical factors in the effective and safe use of
VR HMDs in research [15]. VR has expanded beyond entertainment into education and
professional training, including healthcare and other simulation-based domains [28].
As VR applications become more advanced, users need to complete a wider range of
tasks within virtual environments, such as selecting menus, interacting with objects,
finding information, communicating, and entering text. Among these tasks, text entry
remains challenging because its efficiency and accuracy can be affected by the type of
feedback provided to users[31].
1.1.2 Text Entry in Virtual Reality
Text entry is a fundamental component of human–computer interaction. Users may
need to enter search terms, passwords, messages, commands, form responses, or longer
pieces of written content. In conventional desktop environments, these tasks are
commonly performed using a physical keyboard. However, physical keyboards are not
always suitable for VR environments because a head-mounted display may prevent users
from directly viewing their hands and the keyboard. Physical keyboards also require
additional hardware and a supporting surface, potentially restricting users’ movement
within the virtual environment.
Virtual keyboards provide an alternative by presenting a keyboard layout directly within
the virtual environment. The familiar QWERTY layout allows users to leverage the
typing experience and skills they have acquired from using physical and touchscreen

1.2 Problem Statement
A range of text-entry solutions has been developed for virtual reality, including physical
keyboards, controller-based virtual keyboards, dwell-based selection, direct tapping,
hand-tracked input, gesture typing, eye gaze, and speech recognition. These methods
differ in the hardware they require, the way users select characters and whether text is
entered at character or word level. This section focuses primarily on virtual QWERTY
keyboard methods that are directly related to the six techniques implemented in this
project.
1.2.1 Existing VR Text-Entry Solutions
Controller-based pointing is a common approach to VR text entry. A tracked handheld
controller projects a virtual ray towards the keyboard, allowing the user to highlight a
key and confirm the selection by pressing a trigger or button. A related approach is
dwell-based selection, in which a character is entered automatically after the pointer
remains over a key for a predefined period. Dwell selection removes the need for
repeated button presses but introduces a fixed delay for each character. Controllers can
also be used for direct interaction, where the user moves the controller towards the
keyboard and taps individual virtual keys. These approaches provide different balances
between interaction distance, confirmation effort, and physical movement. Hand-tracked
input removes the need for handheld controllers by using cameras on the VR headset to
estimate the positions and movements of the user’s hands and fingers. Characters may
be selected through fingertip contact, pinching, or direct mid-air tapping. Hand tracking
allows users to interact with virtual keyboards through visible representations of their
own hands and to adapt familiar QWERTY typing behaviours to immersive
environments. However, mid-air input lacks passive haptic feedback and may be affected
by tracking inaccuracies, limited hand visibility, and the design of the selection
mechanism[11][2].
Differences have also been observed between controller-based and hand-tracked input.
Akhoroz and Yildirim[3] compared hand-tracked poke typing with controller-based input

Swipe or word-gesture typing provides an alternative to selecting each character

However, these techniques involve different hardware requirements and interaction
principles. Since the present project examines manual interaction with a virtual
QWERTY keyboard in fully immersive VR, they are not included in the experimental
comparison.
1.2.2 Existing Problems and Limitations
Despite the range of VR text-entry techniques proposed in previous research, their
reported results are often difficult to compare directly. Existing studies vary in the
headsets and tracking systems used, keyboard layouts and dimensions, phrase sets,
training procedures, and evaluation metrics. Accuracy is particularly difficult to
compare because studies may report total, corrected, or uncorrected error rates, or use
different character- and word-level measures. After analysing 176 XR text-entry
techniques, Bhatia et al. [4] identified considerable variation in the performance metrics
reported across previous studies, including the use of different error-rate measures. This
variation makes direct comparison and benchmarking between techniques more difficult.
Consequently, reported differences in performance may reflect both the input techniques
and variations in experimental design.
Virtual keyboards also present interaction challenges that are not encountered to the
same extent with physical keyboards. Mid-air keyboards lack fixed key boundaries,
passive haptic feedback and a surface on which users can rest their hands. Hand-tracked
input may additionally be affected by finger occlusion, tracking inaccuracies and

Different confirmation mechanisms introduce further trade-offs. Trigger-based selection

correspond to multiple words. Its performance therefore depends on trajectory
processing, dictionary coverage, word-frequency information, candidate ranking and the
design of the correction interface.
Previous studies have generally examined only selected parts of this wider design space,
such as controller-based selection, one- or two-finger hand input, or comparisons
between tapping and word-gesture typing. Bhatia et al.[4] also showed that XR
text-entry techniques vary widely in their interaction characteristics and evaluation
approaches. Although these studies provide valuable evidence, differences between their
systems make it difficult to isolate the effects of input device, confirmation mechanism,
interaction distance, and number of active fingers.
The present project addresses this limitation by implementing six text-entry methods
within the same Unity VR application: controller ray selection with trigger
confirmation, controller ray selection with dwell confirmation, controller-based swipe
typing, direct controller tapping, two-index-finger hand-tracked typing, and ten-finger
hand-tracked typing. All methods use the same virtual QWERTY keyboard, phrase set,
timing procedure, and evaluation framework, allowing them to be compared under more
consistent experimental conditions.
1.3 Project Aim
The aim of this project is to design, implement and evaluate six virtual QWERTY
text-entry methods within a shared immersive VR application. The methods, presented
in their experimental order, are controller ray selection with trigger confirmation
(Press), controller-based swipe typing (Swipe), direct controller tapping (StickTap),
controller ray selection with dwell confirmation (Dwell), two-index-finger hand-tracked
typing (HandTouch) and ten-finger hand-tracked typing (HandTouch10).
The study compares the methods in terms of text-entry speed, attempted-prefix

method.

Chapter 2
Background Research
2.1 Introduction
This chapter reviews existing research on virtual keyboard text entry in virtual reality.
It examines controller-based, hand-tracked and swipe-based techniques, compares their
main performance and interaction challenges, and discusses relevant recognition and
evaluation methods. The chapter concludes by identifying the research gap and
explaining the methods selected for this project.
2.2 Existing VR Text-Entry Techniques
VR text-entry techniques can be distinguished according to the input device, the
mechanism for selecting characters, and whether text is entered character by character
or at word level. The following sections review controller-based, hand-tracked and
word-gesture techniques, which are most relevant to the methods implemented in this
project. Other approaches, including physical keyboards, head or gaze input and speech
recognition, are also considered to establish the wider context of VR text entry.
2.2.1 Controller-Based Text Entry
Controller-based text entry typically uses the position and orientation of a tracked
handheld controller to interact with a virtual keyboard. One common implementation
projects a virtual ray from the controller towards the keyboard. The user moves the
controller to highlight a key and presses a trigger or button to confirm the selection.
This approach is conceptually similar to pointing and clicking with a computer mouse
and provides users with explicit control over when each character is entered.
Speicher et al. [26] investigated six selection-based VR text-entry methods, including
controller pointing, controller tapping, head pointing and freehand selection. Their
results showed that pointing with tracked handheld controllers performed better than
the other techniques evaluated in the study. The authors attributed the effectiveness of
controller pointing partly to the stability of tracked controllers and the familiarity of
point-and-select interaction. However, controller pointing still requires users to
repeatedly aim at keys and press a confirmation button, potentially increasing the
number of physical actions required for longer text-entry tasks.

2.2. EXISTING VR TEXT-ENTRY TECHNIQUES9
Boletsis and Kongsvik[6] compared four controller-based methods: raycasting, a
drum-like keyboard, head-directed input and a split controller keyboard. Their study
involved 22 participants and evaluated text-entry rate, accuracy, usability and user
experience. Raycasting and the drum-like keyboard received relatively positive usability
and experiential evaluations, and achieved higher text-entry rates than the head-directed
and split-keyboard methods, suggesting that both remote pointing and direct controller
interaction can provide viable VR text-entry solutions. Nevertheless, the methods
required different movement patterns: raycasting involved repeated aiming, whereas the
drum-like keyboard required users to move the controllers directly towards virtual keys.
Dwell-based selection uses a similar pointing mechanism but removes the need to press a
trigger or a button. A character is entered automatically after the pointer remains over
a key for a predefined duration. Although the study by Yu et al. [32] used head-directed
rather than controller-directed pointing, it illustrates the general trade-off associated
with dwell confirmation. DwellType was slower than button-confirmed TapType and
word-level GestureType because every character required a fixed waiting period. Dwell
selection may nevertheless be useful when repeated button presses are undesirable or
inaccessible[32].
Controllers can also be used for direct tapping. Instead of selecting a key from a
distance, the controller is treated as a stylus or drumstick and moved into contact with
the virtual keyboard. Direct tapping may provide a more spatially immediate form of
interaction than raycasting, but it also requires greater controller movement and reliable
key-activation detection. In the study by Boletsis and Kongsvik[6], some participants
reported that forceful tapping could cause characters to be registered twice, while the
active use of both hands could become tiring. Controller-based methods therefore
present different trade-offs in terms of tracking stability, confirmation effort, interaction
distance, typing accuracy and physical movement.
2.2.2 Hand-Tracked Text Entry
Hand-tracked text entry removes the need for handheld controllers by using cameras or
depth sensors to estimate the positions of the user’s hands and fingers. Virtual keys can
be selected through fingertip contact, mid-air tapping or pinch gestures. This approach
may provide a more direct and natural form of interaction because users operate the
keyboard using representations of their own hands rather than an additional device.
Adhikary and Vertanen[2] demonstrated one- and two-index-finger typing on a mid-air
QWERTY keyboard. Their results also showed that visual keyboard information
remained important despite participants’ familiarity with the QWERTY layout.
The use of multiple fingers may allow users to transfer some behaviours developed
through physical-keyboard experience. However, mid-air typing does not provide a

===== PDF PAGE 17 =====

10CHAPTER 2. BACKGROUND RESEARCH
surface on which the hands can rest, fixed key boundaries or passive tactile
confirmation. Finger movements are therefore less constrained than on a physical
keyboard. Tracking may also become less reliable when fingers overlap, move outside the
camera’s field of view or occlude one another.
The number of active fingers creates an important design trade-off. Dudley et al. [10]
compared two-index-finger and ten-finger typing under mid-air and surface-aligned
conditions. They found that aligning the virtual keyboard with a physical surface
significantly improved text-entry rate. Under mid-air conditions, participants did not
use all ten fingers effectively, and ten-finger typing was slower and produced more errors
than two-index-finger typing. These findings indicate that increasing the number of
active fingers does not necessarily improve text-entry performance.
Dudley et al. [11] examined probabilistic hand-based input methods on a mid-air virtual
QWERTY keyboard. Rather than interpreting every finger position as a completely
certain key selection, probabilistic approaches can account for spatial uncertainty and
use language information to estimate the intended character or word. Such methods are
relevant to hand tracking because small tracking errors or imprecise finger movements
can displace the estimated contact point from the intended key.
2.2.3 Swipe and Word-Gesture Typing
Swipe typing, also known as word-gesture typing, enables users to enter an entire word
through one continuous movement. Instead of selecting every character separately, the
user moves a finger, controller or pointer across the approximate locations of the letters
in the intended word. A recognition algorithm compares the recorded path with possible
word trajectories and returns one or more candidate words.
Word-level interaction may reduce the number of discrete selection and confirmation
actions required for text entry. However, the output depends on software interpretation
rather than a direct one-to-one relationship between actions and characters. Different
words may produce similar gesture paths, while variations in movement speed, pointer
position and tracking accuracy can cause repeated gestures for the same word to follow
different trajectories. Swipe interfaces therefore normally require a dictionary, a
trajectory-matching procedure, language-model information and a mechanism for
selecting or correcting predicted words.
Yu et al. [32] compared head-directed tapping, dwell selection and gesture typing.
GestureType achieved a higher entry rate than both character-level techniques in the
initial study, and participants reached a higher rate after further training and
improvements to the recognition algorithm. This finding suggests that word-level
gestures can increase throughput when recognition is sufficiently reliable. However, the
study used head movements, meaning that its results cannot be directly generalised to

Spiess et al. [27] introduced a controller-operated word-gesture keyboard based on direct
interaction with a virtual keyboard. Their system adapted the continuous gesture
principle used on touchscreen keyboards to VR, allowing users to move a tracked
controller across the keyboard to form word trajectories. The preliminary evaluation
reported promising usability results for controller-based word-gesture input, although
further evaluation was required to establish its performance relative to other VR
text-entry methods.
Kern et al.[14] subsequently compared contact-based tapping and word-gesture
keyboards in VR and video see-through AR with 64 participants. Both keyboards were
operated using tracked handheld controllers, and both techniques supported text
correction, word suggestions, capitalisation and punctuation. In the VR condition, the
tap keyboard achieved a higher entry rate than the word-gesture keyboard. It also
received higher usability and user-experience ratings and produced a lower task load.
Nevertheless, participants demonstrated a significant learning effect after typing only
ten sentences in each condition. These results indicate that swipe typing does not
necessarily outperform tapping, as its effectiveness depends on the recognition method,
interface design, training and correction process.
Swipe typing is particularly relevant to the present project because it differs
fundamentally from the other methods. The trigger-based, dwell-based, direct
controller-tapping and hand-tracked tapping methods implemented in this project
operate primarily through discrete character selections, whereas the controller-based
swipe method converts a continuous trajectory into a predicted word. Its evaluation
must therefore consider not only text-entry speed and error rate, but also recognition
accuracy, candidate selection and the cost of correcting an incorrect prediction.
2.2.4 Other VR Text-Entry Approaches
Beyond manual interaction with mid-air virtual keyboards, recent studies have
investigated alternative text-entry approaches for VR environments, including
gaze-based input and speech recognition. Bhatia et al. [4] compiled and analysed a
database of 176 text-entry techniques across the broader field of XR, including VR, and
highlighted variations in technique design, evaluation metrics and interaction attributes.
Gaze and speech provide alternative methods that reduce reliance on handheld input.
Mansour and Orlosky[19] investigated a gaze-based swipe technique in which users
entered words by looking at virtual keys in sequence to trace a word path. Adhikary and
Vertanen[1] found that speaking sentences and correcting recognition errors through a
mid-air keyboard was faster and more accurate than keyboard-only input. The method
still requires an effective mechanism for correcting recognition errors. In practical

2.3 Challenges and Comparative Analysis
VR text-entry techniques involve trade-offs among speed, accuracy, correction effort,
physical demand and usability. Hardware, keyboard design, recognition methods,
training and evaluation procedures also affect reported performance.
2.3.1 Text-Entry Speed, Accuracy and Correction
Text-entry speed is commonly reported in WPM, although results vary across interaction
methods. Comparative studies have reported strong performance for controller pointing.
Speicher et al.[26] found that tracked-controller pointing outperformed the other
selection-based techniques evaluated in their study. Boletsis and Kongsvik[6] reported
satisfactory entry rates and positive usability results for raycasting and drum-like
controller interaction, alongside moderate error rates. Controller performance therefore
depends on the design of the pointing and confirmation mechanisms.
In Study 1, Yu et al. [32] reported final-session mean entry rates of 10.59 WPM for
DwellType, 15.58 WPM for TapType, and 19.04 WPM for GestureType after six
sessions. In Study 2, which examined GestureType with an improved recognition
algorithm, the mean entry rate increased from 17.04 WPM in the first session to 24.73
WPM in the eighth and final session after approximately 60 minutes of training. Session
had no significant effect on either the word-level corrected or uncorrected error rate[32].
Hand-tracked techniques can achieve competitive entry rates with visual feedback and
error-tolerant decoding. Adhikary and Vertanen[2] reported approximately 16 WPM for
one- and two-index-finger typing on a visible mid-air QWERTY keyboard, with final
post-recognition character error rates below 2%. With an invisible keyboard providing
minimal visual feedback, entry speed fell to approximately 11 WPM and the error rate
increased to 3.3%. Visual guidance therefore remained important despite participants’
familiarity with QWERTY.
Dudley et al. [11] examined probabilistic hand-based touch and gesture typing. They
reported typical entry rates of 20–30 WPM and average peak rates of 40–45 WPM.
These results show the performance potential of probabilistic decoding under imprecise
mid-air input and tracking uncertainty. Hand-tracked performance depends on both the

physical interaction and the decoder’s ability to infer the intended text. Kern et al. [14]
compared contact-based tapping and word-gesture keyboards in VR and video
see-through AR. In VR, tapping was faster, received higher usability and
user-experience ratings, and produced a lower task load. Participants also showed a
significant learning effect across the ten sentences typed in each condition. In Kern et
al.’s implementation, word-gesture typing did not outperform tapping, although its
performance changed after a short period of practice. Accuracy is difficult to represent
using a single measure. Studies report different combinations of uncorrected errors,
corrected errors, backspace use and decoder performance[4]. A low final character error
rate may conceal substantial correction effort. An incorrect swipe prediction can be
corrected before submission but may require candidate inspection, alternative selection,
deletion or gesture repetition. Evaluation should distinguish final transcription accuracy
from the frequency and cost of correction. The six methods implemented in this project
use different confirmation and correction actions. The formal evaluation therefore
includes Gross WPM, attempted-prefix CER, derived accuracy, backspace count,
candidate-confirmation count and total physical actions. For Swipe, recognition quality
and candidate-selection usability are assessed through participant ratings. Objective
Top-1 and Top-5 recognition accuracy is reported only for the separate developer
validation described in Section 4.5.4.
2.3.2 Feedback, Tracking and Physical Demand
Mid-air virtual keyboards lack fixed key boundaries, hand support and passive haptic
feedback. Users therefore rely more heavily on visual feedback and designed haptic cues,
which can affect spatial awareness, visual attention and typing performance.
Dudley et al. [11] identified the lack of passive haptic feedback, unconstrained
movement, tracking inaccuracies and observability limitations as sources of imprecise
mid-air input. Probabilistic decoding can mitigate input noise while the underlying
sensing constraints remain. Dudley et al. [10] also found that ten-finger mid-air typing
was slower and produced more errors than two-index-finger typing.
Chellali et al. [9] compared hover and keypress visual feedback across three passive
haptic conditions. Keypress feedback increased typing speed and reduced workload,
while hover feedback reduced errors and slowed entry. Participants spent more time
looking at the keyboard with keypress feedback and without haptic support. Feedback
design therefore affects typing speed, accuracy, workload and visual attention.
Chellali et al. [9] also found that the physical-keyboard condition increased the error
rate. The authors attributed this result to possible tracking inaccuracies and
misalignment between the physical and virtual keyboards. The effectiveness of passive
haptic feedback may therefore depend on calibration and tracking reliability.

Controller-based methods introduce different movement demands. Raycasting requires
repeated aiming and confirmation, while direct controller tapping requires movement
towards the keyboard. Boletsis and Kongsvik[6] reported satisfactory entry rates and
positive usability results for raycasting and drum-like input. Some participants
described the active two-handed movements as tiring, although tiredness was not a
major complaint.
Souchet et al.[25] identified visual fatigue, muscular fatigue and mental overload as
relevant risks for prolonged workplace use of immersive VR. Text-entry evaluation
should therefore consider posture, arm movement, exposure duration and visual
attention alongside transcription performance.
Objective and subjective measures capture complementary aspects of performance.
WPM and error rates quantify text-entry output, while workload, fatigue, usability and
preference assess its physical and cognitive cost. A small speed advantage may offer
limited practical value when it requires greater arm movement, sustained visual
attention or frequent correction.
2.3.3 Limitations of Existing Comparisons
Existing studies examine different parts of the VR text-entry design space. Speicher et
al. [26] compared six selection-based techniques. Boletsis and Kongsvik[6] evaluated four
controller-based methods. Adhikary and Vertanen[2] examined one- and
two-index-finger input across different visual keyboard designs. Dudley et al. [11]
studied probabilistic hand-based touch and gesture input. Kern et al. [14] compared
tapping and word-gesture typing in VR and video see-through AR. These distinct scopes
limit direct comparison across the input devices and interaction mechanisms considered
in the present project.
Experimental conditions also vary in the headsets and tracking technologies used,
keyboard dimensions, interaction distances, visual feedback, correction support, phrase
sets, training and participant characteristics. Reported performance may reflect both
the input method and the experimental setup. Training duration and decoder support
can also affect results. Additional practice may increase entry speed, and automatic
decoding can improve final accuracy under imprecise input[11]. Speicher et al. [26]
noted that methodological differences limited direct comparison of performance
estimates from previous studies.
Performance measures also vary across studies. Researchers may report total error rate,
corrected errors, uncorrected errors, backspace use, workload or usability. Gesture-based
systems may also measure recognition accuracy and candidate-selection behaviour,
making their metric sets less comparable with character-level techniques. Bhatia et al.
[4] documented substantial variation in the metrics used to evaluate 176 XR text-entry

techniques, highlighting the need for more consistent reporting.
Learning effects create another source of variation. Kern et al. [14] reported a significant

2.4 Swipe Recognition Methods
Swipe typing uses a continuous movement trajectory to infer the intended word. The
recorded gesture provides no one-to-one record of selected keys and may vary with
movement speed, tracking noise and typing behaviour. Recognition systems therefore
process the trajectory, generate word predictions and rank likely candidates.
2.4.1 Trajectory Processing and Matching
A swipe gesture can be represented as a sequence of two- or three-dimensional points
recorded as the user moves across a virtual keyboard. In VR, trajectory patterns vary
with interaction mode, keyboard size, visual feedback, user behaviour and tracking
noise. Decoders therefore transform the recorded data before prediction.
Shen et al.[23] developed a neural decoder for an optical see-through AR headset that
translated three-dimensional fingertip trajectories directly into text. Feature engineering
and data augmentation supported decoding under variable mid-air input and limited
training data. Gesture2Text later transformed continuous trajectories into coarsely
discretised spatial representations and pre-trained a neural decoder that generalised
across mid-air and on-surface word-gesture keyboards in AR and VR[24].
Two common approaches are template matching and neural decoding.
Template-matching methods are easy to configure but susceptible to noisy trajectories.
Conventional neural decoders can improve prediction accuracy but require suitable
training data and deep-learning expertise[24].

===== PDF PAGE 23 =====

2.4.2 Candidate Generation and Ranking
After trajectory processing, the decoder estimates the intended word. Similar words
may produce similar paths, creating uncertainty among candidate predictions.
Probabilistic methods can combine uncertain spatial observations with linguistic
information to support error-tolerant decoding[11].
A predefined vocabulary can reduce the search space before ranking. Candidate scores
may combine trajectory, spatial and contextual evidence. In the AR-focused
RingGesture system, score fusion combined a word-gesture decoder, a spatial
spelling-correction model and a lightweight contextual language model to rank
predictions[22]. Gesture2Text reported Top-1 and Top-4 prediction accuracy for its
pre-trained decoder[24].
The highest-ranked word may be accepted as the default prediction, with alternatives
provided for correction. Top-1 and Top-k accuracy measure decoder performance.
Candidate-selection frequency and correction actions capture the effort required to
resolve inaccurate predictions. Chapter 4 describes the trajectory-processing and
candidate-ranking methods implemented in this project.
2.5 Evaluation Methods for VR Text Entry
VR text-entry evaluation combines objective measures of speed and accuracy with
subjective measures of workload and user experience. Training, condition order and
phrase selection also require control.
2.5.1 Objective Performance Measures
WPM measures text-entry speed. Character error rate (CER) and word error rate
(WER) quantify differences between the target and submitted text. Recent studies have
used WPM and error-based measures to evaluate hand-tracked, tapping and
word-gesture techniques[2][11][14].
Final accuracy does not capture corrections made before submission. Shen et al. [23]
reported uncorrected and corrected CER to distinguish the predicted text before
correction from the text submitted after user correction. Backspace count, correction
actions and total input actions can provide additional measures of correction effort.
Swipe decoders can also be evaluated using recognition-specific measures. Gesture2Text
reported Top-1 and Top-4 prediction accuracy[24]. Candidate-selection frequency and
gesture re-entry can capture the interaction cost of incorrect predictions.

2.5.2 Workload, Fatigue and User Experience
Subjective measures capture demands that are not reflected in typing speed and
accuracy. A fast technique may still require substantial mental effort, physical
movement or visual attention.
Wang et al. [29] found that Trace was more efficient but produced greater frustration
and cognitive workload. However, among participants familiar with VR, the frustration
and cognitive-load levels associated with Trace were reduced to approximately the same
level as those associated with Tap.
These studies support combining workload, perceived physical exertion, usability and
user-experience measures with objective performance. The present study also measures
physical fatigue and user preference to assess the practical demands of each method.
2.5.3 Experimental Evaluation Design and Phrase Sets
Comparative VR text-entry studies often use input method as a within-subject factor,
allowing each participant to test every method. Counterbalancing controls order effects,
and equal practice reduces differences in familiarity.
Wang et al. [29] randomly selected separate practice and experimental phrases from the
MacKenzie and Soukoreff phrase set for each method and participant. They balanced
the method order using a Latin square design. Kern et al. [14] selected ten sentences of
similar length, randomised their presentation order and counterbalanced the tap and
swipe conditions. Kern et al. [14] also reported a significant learning effect across the
ten sentences per condition, showing the importance of consistent practice and clear
reporting of training procedures. The present study uses a fixed method order for
operational reasons. The input method and its sequence position are therefore
confounded. This limits causal comparison between methods.
Keyboard dimensions, target-text presentation, timing rules and correction functions
should remain consistent unless they are experimental variables. Phrase sets should be
matched for length and difficulty. Pilot testing can confirm suitable task durations,
practice periods and rest intervals.
2.6 Research Gap and Project Positioning
Recent studies have evaluated controller-based, hand-tracked and word-gesture
text-entry techniques in immersive environments[11][14]. Cross-study comparison
remains difficult because devices, keyboard designs, training procedures, phrase sets and
evaluation measures vary. Reported differences may therefore reflect both the input
technique and the experimental design[4].

===== PDF PAGE 25 =====

18CHAPTER 2. BACKGROUND RESEARCH
Previous studies have examined separate components of immersive text entry, including
tapping and word-gesture input[14], probabilistic hand-based input[11],
three-dimensional trajectory decoding[23] and feedback design[9]. Evidence comparing
these factors within one experimental framework remains limited.
The present project responds to this gap through a shared-platform within-subjects
comparison of six input methods implemented on the same virtual keyboard. The design
standardises the application, layout, phrase presentation and measurement procedure.
2.7 Choice of Methods
This project compares six VR text-entry methods within the same Unity application:
controller ray selection with trigger confirmation, controller-based swipe typing, direct
controller tapping, controller ray selection with dwell confirmation, two-index-finger
hand-tracked typing and ten-finger hand-tracked typing. These methods were selected to
represent a range of input modalities and interaction designs, including controller-based
and hand-tracked input, remote and direct interaction, different confirmation
mechanisms, and, for the hand-tracked conditions, different numbers of active fingers.
All conditions use the same virtual QWERTY layout, target-phrase presentation, timing
procedure, and visual design. Method-specific feedback is provided where required by
each interaction technique. The study employs a within-subjects design, with all
participants completing the conditions in a fixed order. Practice trials are provided
before each condition to familiarise participants with the corresponding interaction
technique. Although the within-subjects design reduces the influence of individual
differences, the fixed condition order may introduce learning, fatigue, and order effects;
this is therefore acknowledged as a limitation of the study.
The evaluation measures typing speed, attempted-prefix accuracy, correction behaviour,
physical actions, workload, physical fatigue, usability and user preference. For
controller-based Swipe typing, the formal user study additionally evaluates perceived
recognition quality and candidate-selection usability through participant ratings.
2.8 Chapter Summary
This chapter reviewed controller-based, hand-tracked and swipe-based VR text-entry
techniques, focusing on speed, accuracy, correction effort, feedback, tracking and physical
demand. It also examined swipe-recognition approaches and evaluation methods. The
review identified limited comparability across existing studies and informed the selection
of six input methods for controlled evaluation within a single Unity VR application.

3.4 Experimental Design
The study uses a within-subjects design. Every participant completes all six methods.
The evaluation uses descriptive comparisons for a target sample of ten participants.
3.4.1 Research Questions
The study addresses four questions:
1. What differences are observed in speed, accuracy and correction behaviour across
the six methods?
2. What differences are observed in workload, fatigue, ease, comfort and satisfaction
across the six methods?
3. How do participants rate swipe recognition and candidate selection?
4. Which methods do participants prefer, and what strengths or problems do they
report?
3.4.2 Participants
The target sample is ten valid participants. All participants complete the six methods in
the same fixed order. Extra recruitment replaces withdrawn, incomplete or technically
invalid sessions.
Participants must be at least 18 years old and able to read English phrases. They must
know the QWERTY layout and have normal or corrected vision. Previous VR
experience is optional.
The background questionnaire records dominant hand, age range and self-rated
keyboard skill. It also records swipe use, VR use, controller-pointing experience,
hand-tracking experience and relevant health conditions.
3.4.3 Controlled Task Settings
Across all six conditions, participants used the same virtual QWERTY keyboard, task
interface, instructions, formal phrase set, phrase order and 120-second time limit. The
keyboard was restored to a common starting position before each condition, although
participants could subsequently adjust its position for comfort.

Participants transcribe English sentences displayed on the screen. The practice array

4.3 Virtual Keyboard Implementation
4.3.1 Key Objects and Contact Probes
Each virtual key contains VRKeyboardKey and a trigger BoxCollider. Only objects
with VRKeyboardPressProbe can activate it. Crossing the press threshold generates one
event; the probe must cross the lower release threshold before another event can occur.
This hysteresis suppresses repeated input during continuous contact. A valid press also
moves the key cap and plays an audio cue. Figure 4.1 shows the press and release
process. Each key converts its press depth into a normalised value between 0 and 1. A
key input is generated when this value reaches 0.85. The key is not re-armed until the
value falls to 0.35 or below. The difference between these thresholds creates a hysteresis
band that prevents repeated input when the probe moves slightly near the activation
point.

4.5 Swipe Recognition and Candidate Interface
4.5.1 Gesture Capture
VRKeyboardSwipeInput records the ray path while the participant holds the trigger.
The path is projected onto the normalised two-dimensional keyboard plane. A new
point is recorded when the ray has moved by at least 0.01, and the path is reduced when
it reaches 256 points. The first touched letter is always accepted. Later letters are
accepted when the ray moves near the key centre or remains on the key for 0.04 seconds.
Staying on the same key for 0.22 seconds records a repeated letter. While the trigger
remains held, a 0.08-second tolerance allows the ray to cross small gaps between keys.
Releasing the trigger completes the gesture, and a valid word gesture is sent to
SwipeTypingRecognizer.
4.5.2 Keyboard Model and Preprocessing
The recogniser uses ideas from earlier word-gesture systems. Each word has a template
based on the centres of its letter keys. The recogniser compares the user’s path with
these templates. It uses the start and end points to remove unlikely words. It also
compares both the shape and the position of the path. These ideas were mainly based
on SHARK²[16].
SwipeKeyboardLayout finds the centres of the 26 letter keys. It projects these points
onto a normalised two-dimensional keyboard plane. The same plane is used for the
user’s swipe path. The recogniser first cleans the recorded path. It removes consecutive
points that are less than 0.004 apart. It then smooths the path by averaging each point
with nearby points. It also removes isolated points that are far from the rest of the
path. A threshold of three standard deviations is used for this step.

the candidate list. The candidate interface allows the participant to resolve this

4.6 Experimental Workflow Implementation
4.6.1 Interface States
InstructionCanvasController manages eight interface states: Introduction, Method
Instruction, Practice Choice, Practice Trial, Formal Ready, Formal Trial, Formal Result
and Completed. Each state controls the visible interface and available user input.
Instruction screens disable text entry, and trial screens enable it. The controller also
keeps the selected input method and practice state consistent with the mode selector,
active sentence set and ResultOutput.
4.6.2 Practice and Formal Trials
TypingTestSession uses two practice sentences and eight formal sentences, as shown in
Figure 4.8. Each trial lasts up to 120 seconds, starting with the first valid input action.
After each input, the system compares the transcription with the target. Incorrect
characters are displayed in red and the current target position in blue. A sentence
completes when the transcription reaches the target length. The session records the
result, clears the field and loads the next sentence. Practice ends after two completed
sentences or timeout; formal entry ends after eight completed sentences or timeout.
Figure 4.8: Practice and formal sentence sets configured in the TypingTestSession com-
ponent.
4.6.3 Result Display and Method Progress
When practice is completed, InstructionCanvasController switches to the formal
sentence set and displays the Formal Ready screen. If practice is skipped, the formal
trial is prepared directly. The trial timer begins with the first valid input action. At the
end of each formal trial, ResultOutput exports the collected data. The result screen then
displays Gross WPM, accuracy derived from attempted-prefix CER, error count and the
project-specific Effective WPM measure. Text entry is disabled during the five-second
result display. The application then advances automatically to the next method.

4.7 Measurement and Data Export
4.7.1 Physical Action Recording
VRKeyboardInputTelemetry records physical actions during active trials: key presses,
swipe gestures, Backspace, Shift, Caps Lock and controller candidate confirmations.
Each swipe contributes one action. Controller confirmation adds a CandidateSelection
action; keyboard confirmation uses its existing key action. ResultOutput stores
aggregate action totals at sentence and trial levels, with separate Backspace, Shift and
Caps Lock fields. The first valid input action also starts the timer.
4.7.2 Sentence and Trial Metrics
TypingTestSession calculates the performance measures defined in Section 3.2.3.
Completed sentences are compared with the full target text using Levenshtein distance.
If the timer expires during a sentence, the entered text is compared with a target prefix
of the same length. The entered part of the sentence is included in the results, while the
unattempted suffix is excluded. Any later sentences that have not been started are also
excluded.
A timed-out sentence with no entered text produces no sentence-level record. A
partially entered sentence produces a record withCompleted=false. Characters entered
beyond the full target length remain insertion errors.
The exportedCERfield therefore stores the attempted-prefix CER defined in Section
3.2.3. Typing progress is measured separately through Gross WPM, total entered
character count and the completion state of each sentence.
4.7.3 CSV Output and Participant IDs
ResultOutput appends trial records to trial_results.csv and sentence records to
sentence_results.csv. Trial rows contain participant ID, method, trial type, duration,
WPM, CER, accuracy, action counts and validity fields. Sentence rows contain target
and entered text, duration, character counts, errors, actions and completion state. For a
partially entered sentence,TargetTextstores the full target sentence, while
TargetCharacterCountstores the length of the target prefix used for comparison. The
CERandAccuracyPercentfields are calculated from this attempted prefix. The
Completedfield distinguishes completed sentences from partially entered sentences.
Session ID and trial ID link both files; participant ID links them to questionnaire data,
and all Participant IDs follow the P001 format.

Chapter 6
Conclusions and Future Work
6.1 Conclusions
This project designed, implemented, and evaluated six virtual QWERTY text-entry
methods within a shared VR application. The system provided a consistent keyboard,
trial procedure, data-recording framework, and questionnaire process for comparing
controller-based, swipe-based, and hand-tracked input. A within-subjects user study
with ten participants produced both objective performance data and subjective feedback
for all six methods.
The evaluation showed that practical VR text entry depends on several factors,
including typing speed, accuracy, correction effort, workload, tracking reliability, and
user preference. Press provided the most dependable overall interaction in the current
implementation. Swipe also showed strong potential for efficient word-level input and
was positively received by participants. Dwell provided stable and accurate input with a
lower typing speed. The hand-tracked methods showed greater dependence on tracking
and contact accuracy. Increasing the number of active fingers did not automatically
improve text-entry performance.
The project therefore demonstrates the value of comparing different interaction
approaches within the same VR keyboard and evaluation framework. Press and Swipe
provide the strongest basis for further development in the current system. The results
also identify tracking, contact detection, keyboard configuration, and word prediction as
important areas for improving future VR text-entry interfaces.
6.2 Future Work
Future work should improve both the input methods and the experimental design.
Swipe recognition could be improved through better candidate ranking, contextual
language information, and a larger vocabulary. The candidate interface could also use
controller-ray pointing for direct word selection. Future logging should record the
intended word, candidate positions, and selected candidate to support objective Top-1
and Top-k recognition measures. StickTap could be improved by adjusting keyboard
layout, key size, spacing, and overall scale. These changes may reduce unintended
activations and improve contact precision. The controller-probe position and length

could also be easier to adjust for individual users. Hand-tracked typing requires better
tracking and contact detection. Fingertip confidence, contact rejection, individual
calibration, adaptive activation thresholds, and a surface reference could improve
stability and reduce unintended key presses. These improvements are particularly
relevant to HandTouch10, which showed large differences between participants. Previous
research also indicates that surface alignment can improve mid-air typing and that more
active fingers do not necessarily improve performance. Future studies should use a larger
and more diverse sample, counterbalanced method orders, longer practice periods, and
matched phrase sets. The current fixed sequence does not separate method effects from
sequence position. Longer sessions and more realistic tasks could also be evaluated,
including search queries, messages, passwords, forms, and longer text. These tasks would
help identify where character-level, word-level, and hand-tracked input are most suitable.

Project Information Sheet 
Project Title: Design and Evaluation of Six Virtual Reality Text-Entry Methods 
Information sheet date: 20.07.2026 
You are invited to take part in a student research project. Before deciding, please read this 
information carefully. It explains why the study is being carried out and what participation involves. 
You may discuss it with others and ask any questions before deciding whether to take part. 
Project aim and duration: This project investigates six ways of entering text on a virtual QWERTY 
keyboard in immersive virtual reality: controller ray input with trigger confirmation (Press), controller 
swipe typing (Swipe), direct controller tapping (StickTap), controller ray input with dwell 
confirmation (Dwell), two-index-finger hand-tracked typing (HandTouch), and ten-finger hand-
tracked typing (HandTouch10). The study compares typing speed, accuracy, correction behaviour, 
workload, physical fatigue, usability and preference. One session is expected to take approximately 
60 minutes, including instructions, practice, questionnaires and rest breaks. 
Why have I been chosen? You are eligible if you are aged 18 or over, can read English phrases, are 
familiar with the QWERTY keyboard layout, and have normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Previous 
VR experience is not required. Approximately 10 participants will be recruited. 
Do I have to take part? 
No. Taking part is voluntary. If you decide to participate, you will receive this information sheet and 
be asked to sign a consent form. You may decline any question, take a break, or stop the session at 
any time without giving a reason and without negative consequences. You may request withdrawal 
of your identifiable research data until 20.08.2026 by contacting Junxian Li at 
jmhz0887@leeds.ac.uk and providing your participant code. After the data have been 
anonymised and combined with other participants' data, it may no longer be possible to remove 
them. 
What will happen if I take part? You will complete a short background questionnaire and receive a 
VR safety briefing. You will then wear a VR headset and try all six text-entry methods in a fixed order: 
Press, Swipe, StickTap, Dwell, HandTouch and HandTouch10. Before each method, the system will 
show instructions and you will type two practice sentences. You will then complete a formal typing 
trial lasting up to 120 seconds. The same virtual keyboard and task procedure are used for all 
methods. After each method, you will complete a short workload and experience questionnaire. 
Swipe has a few additional questions about word prediction. At the end, you will rank the methods 
and may provide comments. You may rest between methods. 
Are there any risks or benefits? VR may cause temporary eye strain, tiredness, dizziness, nausea, 
headache, or hand and arm discomfort. Tell the researcher immediately if you feel uncomfortable. 
The headset will be removed and the session paused or stopped. There is no guaranteed personal 
benefit, but your participation may help improve understanding of VR text-entry design.

Appendix B
Ethical Issues Addressed
The main ethical considerations in this project concerned informed consent, participant
safety, privacy, and data management.
All participants received a Participant Information Sheet before taking part and
provided written informed consent. Participation was voluntary. Participants could
decline any question, take a break, or stop the study at any time without giving a
reason. They could also request withdrawal of their identifiable research data before the
stated withdrawal deadline.
The study involved the use of an immersive VR headset, which may cause fatigue,
discomfort, dizziness, or cybersickness. These risks were explained before the
experiment. Participants were encouraged to take breaks when needed and could stop
the session if they experienced discomfort. Practice periods were provided before each
formal trial to allow participants to become familiar with the interaction methods.
Participant privacy was protected through pseudonymous participant IDs. Names were
not stored in the application data or questionnaires, and consent forms were stored
separately from the research data. Participants were not identified in the dissertation or
reported results. Research data were stored securely in accordance with the approved
ethics and data-management procedures.
The study collected only information required for the evaluation of the VR text-entry
methods. This included performance measures, questionnaire responses, and participant
comments. The data were used only for the purposes described in the participant
information and consent documents.

•The word list used by the Swipe recogniser was compiled and edited from
google-10000-english.txtand20k.txtin theGoogle 10000 Englishrepository
by J. Kaufman.
–Available at:https://github.com/first20hours/google-10000-english
•The project was created using Unity’s VR project template and includes sample
assets supplied with XR Interaction Toolkit 3.1.2 and XR Hands 1.5.1.
These resources were used in addition to the Unity packages and libraries referenced in
the report: OpenXR Plugin 1.14.1, XR Interaction Toolkit 3.1.2, XR Hands 1.5.1, Input
System 1.13.1, Universal Render Pipeline 14.0.9 and TextMesh Pro 3.0.9.
